\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reframed the cross-residual GNN study around biomolecular graph representation learning rather than generic benchmark breadth. The final result is a narrower but stronger claim: cross-residual interaction is useful, yet residual variants remain the strongest default family in the completed archive.

Accordingly, the main contribution is not a universally dominant model, but a controlled architecture family that makes these trade-offs visible. Residual models win on PROTEINS, DD, ENZYMES, and AIDS, while cross-residual models win on MUTAG and Mutagenicity; across the broader suite, the best cross variant still improves over the plain baseline on most datasets. The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore a dataset-dependent advantage for cross-residual routing, not a universal superiority claim over residual reuse.

For the topic-facing biological interpretation, the most relevant datasets remain PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES. On these benchmarks, GraphResGNN is strongest on PROTEINS and ENZYMES, whereas NodeResGNN is strongest on DD. Cross variants still provide useful gains over plain stacking, especially on ENZYMES, but they do not replace residual baselines as the default point of reference. This is the right level of claim for the current evidence and provides a credible methodological basis for future plant-oriented graph inference, where preserving both local functional motifs and graph-level biological context is equally important.

There are three immediate directions for future work. First, the topic fit can be strengthened by extending the same cross-residual design to richer protein and gene prediction settings. Second, the current graph-classification backbone can be extended toward plant-related graph inference tasks, especially plant protein identification and functional annotation settings, so that the architectural motivation outlined in the introduction is tested under explicit plant biological supervision. Third, the architecture-level findings reported here can be paired with stronger biological features, including sequence-derived embeddings, ontology signals, expression measurements, or other omics-derived inputs, to move beyond structure-only benchmarks.
