\section*{Discussion}

The main experimental message is precise. Cross-residual routing is usually beneficial relative to plain stacking, but the gain over residual baselines is conditional and dataset-dependent. The present evidence therefore supports CR-GNN as a meaningful architectural design space rather than a universal winner.

The distinction between node-level and graph-level information reuse is also practically important. Node-level cross-residual exchange is more stable across datasets, whereas graph-level cross routing achieves stronger gains only on selected tasks. This suggests that the place where information is reused matters at least as much as the existence of additional reuse paths.

From a biological-application perspective, the strongest evidence still comes from PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES. These datasets support the claim that CR-GNN is useful for biomolecular graph representation learning, but they do not yet justify stronger claims about direct biological discovery or fully integrative omics prediction. The current study remains structure-centric rather than omics-integrative.

This limitation also clarifies the next research step. The architecture-level findings should be tested with richer biological inputs, such as sequence-derived embeddings, ontology annotations, or expression-related signals. In parallel, the same information-flow designs can be extended toward more explicit plant-oriented graph inference tasks so that the methodological motivation in the introduction is examined under domain-specific supervision instead of benchmark transfer alone.
