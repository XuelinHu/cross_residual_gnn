\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.} Graph neural networks are widely used for graph classification on proteins and molecules, but deeper message passing can blur discriminative local structure, and standard skip connections do not test whether cross-branch historical exchange offers a better trade-off than ordinary residual reuse. This issue is especially relevant in biomolecular graph classification, where useful evidence may depend on both local motifs and graph-level context.

\textbf{Methods.} We study Cross-Residual Graph Neural Networks (CR-GNN), a controlled model family that compares plain stacking, node-level residual reuse, node-level cross-residual exchange, graph-level residual propagation, and graph-level cross-summary exchange under shared graph-classification backbones. The final benchmark uses six datasets, nine methods, four message-passing operators, and stratified five-fold evaluation. We further organize targeted experiments on gate control, residual routing modes, residual-parameter sweeps, and exploratory sensitivity scans.

\textbf{Results.} The final results show that residual reuse remains the stronger default family across the biomolecular benchmark package, while cross-residual routing usually improves over plain stacking and yields selective gains on specific datasets. The strongest graph-level residual line remains best on several core settings, but residual routing design materially affects the outcome: mild sparse routing is often competitive and is the strongest family on three of four representative residual-routing targets.

\textbf{Conclusions.} CR-GNN is best interpreted as a biomolecular graph-classification design space rather than a universally dominant architecture. The empirical evidence supports a constrained conclusion: cross-residual interaction is useful but conditional, and residual routing strategy is an important determinant of performance.
\end{abstract}
