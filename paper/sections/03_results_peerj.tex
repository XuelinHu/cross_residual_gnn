The benchmark evaluates six datasets and five CR-GNN architectures under GCNConv with five outer folds. % ============================================================
\subsection{When does each reuse topology help?}
% ============================================================

The dataset-level results reveal two distinct regimes in which different reuse topologies excel.

\textbf{Regime 1 --- graph-level context is discriminative.} On PROTEINS and Mutagenicity, GraphResGNN and GraphCrossGNN occupy the top two positions. These datasets share the property that global graph-level structure carries strong discriminative signal, and GraphResGNN's sequential block design, which reinjects pooled graph summaries at each stage, is well-suited to this regime.

\textbf{Regime 2 --- local structural motifs are discriminative.} On MUTAG---small molecular graphs averaging 18 nodes---NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN are the strongest CR-GNN variants, while GraphResGNN ranks last. The same pattern appears on DD, the largest and sparsest protein graph in the core: both cross-residual variants outperform both residual variants. In these settings, aggressive graph-level representational reshaping appears to suppress the local structural signals that matter most.

\textbf{ENZYMES} stands apart: all architectures perform poorly (best accuracy 0.3333, only $\sim$16 pp above the 6-class random baseline), reflecting the difficulty of learning from 600 graphs with 3-dimensional features across 6 classes.

\subsection{The mechanism: representational continuity vs.\ performance}
% ============================================================

Why does GraphResGNN---which deliberately alters representations between blocks---outperform architectures that preserve them more faithfully? To answer this, we track hidden-state similarity (CKA and cosine) and per-layer gradient norms from $L=1$ to $L=8$ on PROTEINS under GCNConv.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_cka_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{CKA similarity between adjacent depth stages across $L=1$--$8$. Each subplot shows one model.}
\label{fig:layer_cka_comparison}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_cosine_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{Cosine similarity between adjacent depth stages across $L=1$--$8$.}
\label{fig:layer_cosine_comparison}
\end{figure}

Figures~\ref{fig:layer_cka_comparison} and~\ref{fig:layer_cosine_comparison} reveal a counter-intuitive pattern. At $L=8$, the CKA ordering is NodeResGNN (0.968) $>$ GraphCrossGNN (0.952) $>$ PlainGNN (0.951) $>$ NodeCrossGNN (0.946) $>$ GraphResGNN (0.942). The architecture that preserves representations most faithfully (NodeResGNN) wins only 3 of 24 combinations; the architecture that reshapes them most aggressively (GraphResGNN) wins 12 of 24. We term this the \textbf{CKA--performance paradox}: stronger layer-wise representational continuity does not predict stronger final accuracy.

This paradox has a structural explanation. GraphResGNN's three graph-conditioned blocks each receive the previous block's pooled graph embedding as a conditioning signal. This deliberate, structured reshaping---reinjecting accumulating global context at each stage---produces more discriminative final representations precisely \emph{because} representations change substantially between blocks. Conversely, NodeResGNN's faithful preservation of representations across depth may limit its capacity to adapt to task-specific structure.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_gradient_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{Deepest-layer gradient L2 norm across $L=1$--$8$ (PROTEINS, GCNConv).}
\label{fig:layer_gradient_comparison}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:layer_gradient_comparison} confirms that PlainGNN's deepest gradient norm degrades fastest with depth; the residual family maintains more stable gradients throughout. NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN exhibit comparable gradient stability, confirming that cross-branch exchange does not introduce gradient interference.

The trade-off is now quantitative. GraphResGNN wins most often (12/24) but with the weakest rank stability (std 1.46)---it can win big or lose badly. GraphCrossGNN wins only once yet is the most stable performer (std 0.75), never falling below fourth. NodeCrossGNN occupies the middle: second-most wins (6/24), intermediate stability (std 1.00), and dominance on DD specifically.

\subsubsection{Gate ablation}

\input{../md/peerj_gate_tables.tex}
\input{../md/peerj_gate_fold_table.tex}
\input{../md/peerj_cross_gate_full_table.tex}

Gate ablations test whether adaptive control of residual injection explains the architectural differences observed above. On the strongest graph-level residual target (\texttt{AIDS + GraphResGNN + GINConv}), the learnable gate performs best, though fixed coefficient 0.5 is close. On six cross-favored targets, learnable and fixed gates each win three---cross-residual architectures are robust to gate configuration, consistent with their greater rank stability.

\subsubsection{Residual routing}

\input{../md/peerj_residual_tables.tex}

Four representative targets compare learnable dense, top-k, and sparse routing modes. Sparse routing (threshold 0.05) is optimal on three of four targets; learnable dense routing remains best only on the strongest graph-level residual target. Two of the three sparse-favoring targets involve cross-residual architectures, suggesting that sparse filtering and cross-branch exchange are complementary.

\subsubsection{Statistical tests}

The paired-test matrices (Tables~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_main}--\ref{tab:statistical_tests_supp}) confirm that most architecture-level gaps are modest relative to fold-level variation.

\subsubsection{Parameter sweep}

Additional top-k ratios and sparsity levels refine rather than change the pattern. The strongest top-k ratio varies by target, and aggressive sparsity (0.10) damages the graph-level residual target, confirming that sparsity benefits are threshold-dependent.
