\section*{Discussion}

The main empirical message is now more precise than in the earlier draft. Cross-residual routing is usually beneficial relative to plain stacking, but the gain over residual baselines is conditional and dataset-dependent. The present evidence therefore supports CR-GNN as a meaningful biomolecular graph-classification design space rather than as a universally dominant model family. This framing is important for interpreting both the strengths and the limits of the paper: the contribution lies in identifying a reproducible hierarchy of reuse mechanisms, not in claiming a new universal best model.

\subsection*{Cross-residual gains are conditional}

The benchmark results do not support the strongest possible claim that cross-residual interaction should replace residual reuse as the default choice. Instead, they support a narrower statement: cross-residual design is often better than plain stacking and can provide selective gains on specific datasets---particularly where rare discriminative motif preservation matters, as on MUTAG and Mutagenicity---but ordinary residual reuse remains the stronger default family across the full biomolecular package. This narrower conclusion is more convincing because it is aligned with the five-fold mean $\pm$ standard deviation reporting and the pairwise statistical tests (Tables~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_main} and~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_supp}) rather than with isolated best-case gains.

The dataset-level pattern is informative. On MUTAG, where NodeCrossGNN leads, the task involves small molecular graphs (avg.\ 18 nodes) where local structural motifs (e.g., specific chemical substructures) carry strong discriminative signal. Cross-branch exchange may help here by preserving rare motifs that a single branch tends to suppress under repeated propagation---a form of rare discriminative motif preservation through complementary parallel pathways. On Mutagenicity, the larger sample size (4,337 graphs) reduces split-level variance, making the NodeCrossGNN advantage ($+0.014$ over NodeResGNN) more stable though still statistically modest. On AIDS, by contrast, GraphResGNN's strong numerical lead ($0.9160$ vs.\ $0.8735$ for NodeCrossGNN) suggests that when graph-level context dominates, tail-preserving residual routing through sequential graph-conditioned blocks is the more effective strategy. This conditional pattern---cross helps when local motifs are rare and discriminative, residual helps when global context dominates---is the paper's central empirical observation.

\subsection*{Node-level and graph-level reuse behave differently}

The distinction between node-level and graph-level information reuse is also practically important. Node-level cross exchange is the more stable cross-oriented design, whereas graph-level cross routing is more sensitive to dataset regime and does not consistently match the strongest graph-level residual lines. This suggests that the location of reuse is at least as important as the existence of an additional reuse path. Importantly, GraphResGNN and GraphCrossGNN carry more parameters than their node-level counterparts (e.g., GraphResGNN $\approx$51k vs. NodeResGNN $\approx$23k parameters on PROTEINS, Table~\ref{tab:all_winners}), because chaining multiple graph-conditioned blocks increases the total parameter budget. The fact that GraphResGNN's numerical lead does not scale uniformly with its parameter advantage---it ranks first on PROTEINS and ENZYMES but is surpassed by lighter node-level models on DD and MUTAG---suggests that the information-flow topology, rather than parameter count alone, is the more important determinant of relative performance.

\subsection*{Why ENZYMES remains difficult for all architectures}

A notable result is that all models perform poorly on ENZYMES in absolute terms: the best model (GraphResGNN, $0.3117 \pm 0.0746$) is only $\sim$15 percentage points above the 6-class random baseline ($16.7\%$). Several factors likely contribute. First, ENZYMES has only 600 graphs with 3-dimensional input features, making representation learning challenging and the inner validation split (10\%) noisy. Second, the 6-class structure creates a harder decision boundary than the binary tasks that dominate the remaining benchmark suite. Third, the observed fold-level standard deviation ($\pm 0.075$) is the largest in the biological core, indicating high split sensitivity. This does not invalidate the relative ranking---the residual family still leads---but it bounds the strength of any architecture-level conclusion drawn from ENZYMES alone. The ENZYMES results are best interpreted as evidence that tail-preserving residual routing provides relative stability even under noisy, low-data regimes, rather than as evidence that any architecture solves the task well.

\subsection*{Residual routing is part of the mechanism story}

The residual-routing ablations add an important qualification to the main architecture comparison. The question is not only whether residual information should be reused, but also whether it should be reused densely, sparsified, or filtered by top-k selection---a question of selective residual filtering rather than binary reuse. The current results show that routing strategy is target-dependent: learnable dense routing remains strongest on the best graph-level residual line where tail-preserving residual routing benefits from rich historical context, while mild sparse routing is frequently advantageous on cross-oriented settings where suppressing weak residual channels reduces head-pattern dominance and prevents unfiltered cross-branch signal accumulation. This shifts the paper away from a simple architecture ranking exercise and toward a mechanism study in which architecture and routing are interpreted together.

The depth-scaling analysis (Figures~\ref{fig:layer_cka_comparison}--\ref{fig:layer_gradient_comparison}) adds another dimension to this mechanism story. By extending the hidden-state similarity and gradient analyses from $L=4$ to $L=8$ layers, we find that the residual family's advantage in preserving representation continuity is not confined to the default depth used in the main benchmark---it strengthens as depth increases. PlainGNN's adjacent-stage CKA degrades more steeply with depth than that of any residual or cross-residual variant, and its deepest-layer gradient norm declines more rapidly. NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN maintain comparable representation stability across all tested depths, confirming that cross-branch exchange does not introduce destructive interference even under deeper configurations. This depth-scaling evidence supports the paper's central claim that residual reuse is the more robust default strategy, and that cross-residual interaction provides a complementary pathway without undermining the stabilizing effect of residual connections.

\subsection*{Evidence hierarchy and study limitations}

The strongest evidence in the paper comes from the five-fold \texttt{LATEST} benchmark and the five-fold targeted ablations. By contrast, the older single-fold sensitivity scans are useful only for optimization interpretation. They should not be treated as the same class of quantitative evidence. A second limitation is statistical power: the paired significance tests (Tables~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_main} and~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_supp}) show that most architecture-level numerical gaps do not reach $p<0.05$ under five-fold evaluation. With only five paired observations per dataset--operator combination, the tests have limited power to detect small-to-medium effects (Cohen's $d < 0.5$). This does not undermine the paper's bounded conclusions, but it means that the claim ``X is stronger than Y'' should be understood as a numerical ordering observed under controlled conditions rather than a statement of statistical dominance. Future work with more folds or repeated random subsampling would provide sharper pairwise comparisons. A third limitation is baseline scope. The benchmark includes representative residual, propagation, and aggregation baselines, but it is not intended as an exhaustive leaderboard against every recent graph-classification method. The paper is instead designed to support a controlled comparison of reuse topologies under a stable protocol. A fourth limitation is dataset scale and task scope: although the datasets are biomolecular or molecular and the biological core is meaningful, they are still medium-scale benchmark datasets for graph classification. The study therefore supports conclusions about controlled graph-classification behavior, not about large-scale scalability, direct biological discovery, omics integration, or task-specific biological interpretation.

\subsection*{Future directions}

The next research step is to test the same information-flow ideas with richer biological inputs, such as sequence-derived embeddings, ontology annotations, or expression-related signals. Another important extension is to evaluate the same controlled reuse questions on larger graph benchmarks, so that the current mechanism-oriented conclusions can be tested beyond the present medium-scale setting. In parallel, the residual-routing perspective introduced here could be extended to broader biomolecular graph tasks where graph topology must be combined with stronger domain supervision rather than benchmark-only evaluation.
