\section*{Introduction}

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are now a core tool for structure-aware learning on molecules, proteins, and biological interaction systems \cite{kipf2016semi,gilmer2017neural,hamilton2017inductive}. In the biological domain, graph representations are valuable because they preserve both local neighborhood structure and larger relational context, which is critical for tasks such as protein function prediction, protein--protein interface analysis, enzyme classification, and biomolecular interaction modeling. Recent studies have already shown concrete biological impact: graph convolutional models have been used for structure-based protein function prediction \cite{gligorijevic2021structure}, large-scale multispecies protein annotation \cite{you2021deepgraphgo}, and protein--protein interface learning \cite{reau2022deeprankgnn}. The same trend is increasingly visible in plant computational biology, where GNN-style modeling is being explored for plant protein identification, functional annotation, and biological network analysis \cite{suiIdentificationPlantVacuole2023,charulekhaUncoveringComplicatedPlant,baongoImprovingPlantFunctional2025}.

Despite this progress, deep biological graph learning remains difficult. Repeated message passing can over-smooth node states, weaken discriminative local motifs, and reduce intermediate diversity, especially when the graph label depends on multiple coordinated substructures rather than on one dominant pattern. This issue is especially relevant in protein-related graphs and plant-oriented graph inference, where useful evidence may be distributed across local regions, cross-scale interactions, and graph-level summaries. A model that only stacks layers sequentially may underuse such complementary information, while a simple residual skip only re-injects the same branch history and does not test whether cross-branch exchange or graph-level feedback can preserve more useful context.

Modern GNN research has proposed several mechanisms to improve deep training, including residual connections \cite{he2016deep, bresson2017residual}, normalization \cite{zhao2020pair, cai2021graphnorm}, edge dropout \cite{rong2019dropedge}, and multi-scale readout designs such as Jumping Knowledge \cite{xu2018representation}. These methods improve optimization or preserve multi-depth features, but most still operate inside a single branch. In parallel, graph-level pooling and hierarchical modules improve global summarization, yet they do not directly test whether exchanging intermediate states across branches or reusing graph summaries across block boundaries yields a better trade-off for biomolecular graph inference.

This paper studies Cross-Residual Graph Neural Networks (CR-GNN), a unified family of graph classification models that isolates three kinds of information reuse: intra-branch residual accumulation, cross-branch node-state exchange, and graph-level summary propagation across sequential blocks. Rather than presenting one oversized architecture, we formulate a controlled model family that includes plain, residual, and cross-residual variants under the same message-passing backbone. This makes it possible to ask a more useful question than whether one model wins everywhere: when does cross-residual interaction help beyond a simpler residual baseline on biomolecular graphs, and when does residual reuse remain the better default for protein-oriented graph inference?

This framing also clarifies the scope of the study. The present work does not claim to solve full integrative omics modeling or novel biological discovery directly. Instead, it focuses on graph representation learning for a protein-oriented benchmark package spanning PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES, and studies how information reuse can preserve biologically meaningful structure more effectively than plain stacking. In this sense, the paper should be read as a biomolecular graph learning study with a technically motivated extension path toward plant-related graph inference settings in which graph topology can later be combined with richer functional or omics annotations.

\subsection*{Related work}

The motivation of CR-GNN is not only architectural but also domain-specific: biological graph learning often requires preserving local structural cues together with broader graph context. We therefore review the literature from six connected perspectives, starting with biomolecular and plant applications before moving to graph classification, message passing operators, deep-training challenges, hybrid architectures, and cross-layer aggregation.

\subsubsection*{GNNs in biomolecular and plant graph learning}

Biological graph learning has moved well beyond generic graph benchmarks. In biomolecular settings, GNNs have already been used for structure-based protein function prediction \cite{gligorijevic2021structure}, large-scale protein function prediction across species \cite{you2021deepgraphgo}, and protein--protein interface pattern discovery \cite{reau2022deeprankgnn}. These studies show that graph models are practically relevant when the target depends on coordinated structural signals rather than on isolated features. The same pattern is now emerging in plant computational biology. Recent work applies graph-based modeling to plant vacuole protein identification \cite{suiIdentificationPlantVacuole2023}, plant biological-network analysis \cite{charulekhaUncoveringComplicatedPlant}, and plant functional annotation from knowledge graphs \cite{baongoImprovingPlantFunctional2025}. This literature does not validate our exact cross-residual design, but it makes the application setting concrete: in both biomolecular and plant tasks, the model often needs to preserve multi-scale biological information rather than only deepen a single message-passing chain.

Benchmark choice matters in this context. TU datasets such as PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES remain widely used because they are easy to reproduce and still expose meaningful differences in graph scale, sparsity, and classification difficulty \cite{morris2020tudataset}. For the present work, these datasets provide a stable protein-oriented evaluation core while keeping the narrative aligned with real biological graph-learning use cases.

\subsubsection*{Graph classification and message passing}

Graph-level prediction requires aggregating node-level features into fixed-size graph representations. Early graph neural networks \cite{gori2005new} laid the foundation for learning on graph-structured data through recursive message passing. The Message Passing Neural Networks framework \cite{gilmer2017neural} unified various GNN architectures under a common formalism, identifying message and vertex update functions as core components.

For graph classification, readout functions play a crucial role in converting node embeddings to graph-level representations. Simple approaches apply global pooling operations such as mean or sum pooling \cite{duvenaud2015convolutional}. However, these methods may not adequately capture hierarchical structural information. To address this, differentiable pooling methods have been proposed: DiffPool \cite{ying2018hierarchical} learns node cluster assignments to coarsen graphs hierarchically, SAGPool \cite{lee2019self} employs self-attention to identify salient nodes, and LocalPool \cite{cangea2018towards} enables sparse hierarchical classification \cite{liu2022graph,li2024graph}.

Various message passing operators have been developed, each imposing different inductive biases on neighborhood aggregation. Graph Convolutional Networks \cite{kipf2016semi}, GraphSAGE \cite{hamilton2017inductive}, GIN \cite{xu2018powerful}, GAT \cite{velivckovic2017graph}, and graph Transformers \cite{dwivedi2020generalization} provide complementary design choices, but they are usually studied inside homogeneous architectures rather than under a unified comparison of information-flow mechanisms.

\subsubsection*{Deep GNN training and cross-layer reuse}

Training deep GNNs faces fundamental challenges due to representation degradation. Over-smoothing causes node representations to converge toward indistinguishable values as depth increases \cite{li2018deeper, chen2023revisiting, li2023non}, while over-squashing limits the propagation of long-range dependencies through graph bottlenecks \cite{topping2021understanding, alon2020bottleneck}. Residual connections \cite{he2016deep,bresson2017residual}, normalization \cite{zhao2020pair,cai2021graphnorm,li2022learning}, and regularization such as DropEdge \cite{rong2019dropedge} alleviate some of these issues, and recent work has shown that very deep GNNs can be trained under suitable designs \cite{li2021training,oono2020simple}.

Cross-layer connections also aim to capture multi-scale information. Jumping Knowledge \cite{xu2018representation}, Graph U-Net \cite{gao2019graph}, APPNP \cite{klicpera2018combining}, and dense graph designs \cite{du2024densegnn} all improve feature reuse, but most approaches operate within a single branch. Our work instead distinguishes between node-level residual reuse, node-level cross exchange, graph-level residual propagation, and graph-level cross-summary exchange within one shared graph-classification framework.

\subsection*{Contributions}

The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalize CR-GNN as a compact architecture family that compares five biologically relevant graph classification models under one shared backbone: PlainGNN, NodeResGNN, NodeCrossGNN, GraphResGNN, and GraphCrossGNN.
    \item We design a biologically focused evaluation protocol built around a protein-oriented benchmark core (PROTEINS, DD, ENZYMES) plus supplementary robustness datasets, and we use a controlled stratified 5-fold setting to make the architectural comparison more rigorous.
    \item We show that cross-residual design is useful but not universally dominant: residual reuse remains the strongest default, while cross-residual routing consistently improves over plain stacking and yields selective gains that clarify when node-level or graph-level information reuse is most helpful.
\end{itemize}
