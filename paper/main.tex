\title{Cross-Residual Graph Neural Networks for Biomolecular Graph Classification: An Empirical Study of Residual Routing}

\author[1]{Xuelin Hu}
\author[2]{Xiaoqin Fu}

\affil[1]{School of Railway Communication and Signaling Engineering, Liuzhou Railway Vocational Technical College, Liuzhou 545000, China}
\affil[2]{School of Railway Locomotive and Rolling Stock, Liuzhou Railway Vocational Technical College, Liuzhou 545000, China}

\corrauthor[2]{Xiaoqin Fu}{fuxiaoqin@ltzy.edu.cn}

\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.} Graph neural networks are widely used for graph classification on proteins and molecules, but deeper message passing can blur discriminative local structure, and standard skip connections do not clarify whether cross-branch historical exchange offers a better trade-off than ordinary residual reuse. This issue is especially pertinent in biomolecular graph classification, where useful evidence may depend jointly on local structural motifs and graph-level context, and where the choice of information-flow topology can materially affect the quality of learned representations.

\textbf{Methods.} We study Cross-Residual Graph Neural Networks (CR-GNN) as a controlled comparison framework that evaluates plain stacking, node-level residual reuse, node-level cross-residual exchange, graph-level residual propagation, and graph-level cross-summary exchange under shared graph-classification backbones. The benchmark is organized around a biologically meaningful protein-oriented core and a supplementary molecular robustness package, with all results reported under stratified five-fold evaluation using mean best test accuracy and fold-level standard deviation. We further organize targeted experiments on gate control, residual routing modes, and residual-parameter sweeps, along with exploratory sensitivity analyses, to isolate the mechanism behind the observed architectural patterns.

\textbf{Results.} Under stratified five-fold evaluation, residual reuse remains the stronger default family across the biomolecular benchmark package, while cross-residual routing consistently improves over plain stacking and provides selective gains on specific datasets rather than a uniform advantage. Within the biological core, graph-level residual propagation leads numerically on several key protein-oriented benchmarks, whereas node-level residual reuse leads on larger sparse protein graphs. Cross-residual variants emerge as the top-ranked designs on selected molecular datasets where rare discriminative motif preservation is particularly valuable. The targeted routing analyses further reveal that routing design materially affects performance: learnable dense routing remains strongest on the best graph-level residual line, while mild sparse routing is frequently advantageous on cross-oriented targets, indicating that how historical information is filtered before reuse is at least as important as whether a reuse path exists.

\textbf{Conclusions.} CR-GNN is best interpreted as a controlled biomolecular graph-classification design space rather than a universally dominant architecture. The empirical evidence supports a bounded conclusion: cross-residual interaction is useful but conditional, residual reuse remains the stronger default reference, and routing strategy is an important determinant of performance. The contribution lies not in a single winning architecture but in a reproducible comparison framework that reveals when and how residual routing helps.
\end{abstract}
