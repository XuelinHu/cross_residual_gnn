\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section answers two questions. First, does cross-residual information flow improve graph classification beyond plain stacking? Second, when compared against simpler residual reuse, where does the cross design help and where does it remain weaker?

\subsection{Experimental Settings}

We begin with the shared protocol, because the later biological interpretation is only meaningful if model, data, and optimization choices are controlled consistently.

\subsubsection{Model Set}

We compare five architectures under the same GCN-style message-passing backbone:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PlainGNN}: plain stacked propagation without explicit hidden-state reuse
    \item \textbf{NodeResGNN}: single-branch node-level residual reuse
    \item \textbf{NodeCrossGNN}: two-branch node-level cross residual exchange
    \item \textbf{GraphResGNN}: graph-level residual refinement driven by pooled graph summaries
    \item \textbf{GraphCrossGNN}: graph-level cross residual exchange across consecutive block pairs
\end{enumerate}

This comparison isolates the effect of information-flow design while holding the operator family fixed.

\subsubsection{Dataset Scope}

The revised topic-facing discussion centers on the unified biological package introduced in Section~\ref{sec:datasets}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Main biological package}: PROTEINS, DD, ENZYMES
    \item \textbf{Supplementary biological or chemical datasets}: MUTAG, AIDS, Mutagenicity
\end{itemize}

This package separates the protein-oriented main narrative from supplementary robustness evidence and keeps the main text aligned with the biological scope of the paper.

\subsubsection{Data Protocol}

With the benchmark scope fixed, we next define the common evaluation procedure applied to every model.

All reported results use stratified 5-fold graph-level evaluation. Inside each training fold, we construct a stratified validation subset with validation ratio 0.1. Model selection is based on validation loss, and the best checkpoint is evaluated on the held-out test fold.

The stratified design matters because it reduces class-distribution artifacts and exposes architectural differences more reliably.

\subsubsection{Optimization}

Once the split protocol is fixed, the remaining source of variation is the training configuration itself. All models are trained under the same optimization framework with validation-based model selection and conservative regularization. Plain and residual baselines follow the same common training template, while the cross-residual models are allowed modest stability-oriented adjustment within that same regime. This keeps the comparison fair while avoiding optimization failure from dominating the architectural comparison.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility}

All reported runs follow the same reproducible evaluation policy. The important point for the paper is that every architecture is tested under the same data protocol, the same operator backbone, and the same model-selection criterion.

\subsection{Main Findings}

These aggregate results already suggest the central pattern, which can be stated more directly before examining the protein-oriented datasets one by one.

\textbf{Residual reuse remains the strongest default family in the reported suite.} Residual variants win PROTEINS, DD, ENZYMES, and AIDS. This makes residual reuse the safest default baseline under the final protocol. In particular, GraphResGNN is best on PROTEINS ($0.7223 \pm 0.0367$), ENZYMES ($0.3117 \pm 0.0746$), and AIDS ($0.9160 \pm 0.0121$), while NodeResGNN is best on DD ($0.7207 \pm 0.0259$).

\textbf{Cross-residual design is selectively strong, not universally dominant.} Cross variants still win completed datasets such as MUTAG and Mutagenicity. More importantly, the best cross variant beats PlainGNN on most completed datasets. Therefore, cross routing is clearly more than a design curiosity: it is a useful alternative information-flow mechanism. However, it still does not consistently beat the best residual baseline, so the evidence does not support the stronger claim that cross universally outperforms residual reuse.

\textbf{Node-level cross is more reliable than graph-level cross.} Across the completed datasets, NodeCrossGNN outperforms NodeResGNN more often than GraphCrossGNN outperforms GraphResGNN. This suggests that exchanging intermediate node-level states is the more robust cross-residual design in the present study, while graph-level cross routing is more dataset-sensitive.

\textbf{Plain stacking is no longer a credible final baseline.} PlainGNN remains competitive on PROTEINS and DD, but it never wins a dataset in the final suite and is usually surpassed by either a residual or a cross-residual variant. This is important for the paper narrative: the meaningful comparison is no longer cross versus plain, but cross versus the stronger residual family.

\subsection{Topic-Facing Biological Interpretation}

The full-suite pattern is still methodologically useful, but the topic-facing interpretation should be anchored in the protein-oriented core datasets. In that narrower sense, the strongest evidence comes from PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES, because they are the closest proxies in the current paper to protein- and enzyme-centered functional inference.

On \textbf{PROTEINS}, GraphResGNN is clearly strongest, indicating that repeated graph-level residual refinement is the most effective way to preserve useful global structure. The best cross model here is NodeCrossGNN at $0.6963 \pm 0.0433$, which improves slightly over NodeResGNN but remains below both PlainGNN and GraphResGNN.

On \textbf{DD}, NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN are nearly tied, with NodeResGNN retaining a narrow lead. This indicates that node-level hidden-state reuse is important on large sparse protein graphs, but cross exchange does not produce a decisive improvement over simpler residual reuse.

On \textbf{ENZYMES}, cross variants become more attractive. GraphCrossGNN reaches $0.3017 \pm 0.0750$ and NodeCrossGNN reaches $0.3000 \pm 0.0580$, both clearly above PlainGNN at $0.2450 \pm 0.0407$. Even so, GraphResGNN still remains the best model. Therefore, ENZYMES is the cleanest topic-facing dataset for arguing that cross-residual pathways can recover useful information that plain stacking loses, but not for arguing that cross should replace residual baselines outright.

\subsection{Ablation Conclusions}

Taken together, these dataset-level observations can now be condensed into a single ablation message.

The final ablation story is therefore precise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plain $\rightarrow$ Cross}: cross-residual routing is usually beneficial relative to plain stacking
    \item \textbf{Residual $\rightarrow$ Cross}: the gain is conditional and dataset-dependent
    \item \textbf{Node-level versus graph-level}: node-level cross is more stable, while graph-level cross achieves stronger wins only on selected datasets
\end{itemize}

This is the strongest claim that the current evidence supports. Cross-residual design should be presented as a meaningful and reusable architectural design space, not as a universal winner over residual reuse.

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity}

The next question is whether this ablation story is stable under moderate changes in the cross-model hyperparameters.

To complement the structural ablation, we ran a focused parameter-sensitivity study for the two cross-residual models on the topic-facing datasets PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES. We varied depth, dropout, and learning rate under the same general training setting. Figures~\ref{fig:depth_sensitivity}--\ref{fig:lr_sensitivity} summarize the resulting trends.

The sensitivity patterns are dataset-specific rather than uniform. On \textbf{PROTEINS}, NodeCrossGNN is most sensitive to depth, while GraphCrossGNN benefits from lighter regularization. On \textbf{DD}, both cross variants are more optimization-sensitive, and GraphCrossGNN reacts especially strongly to depth. On \textbf{ENZYMES}, however, the scan exposes a different regime: the validation signal is noticeably noisier, which makes local hyperparameter improvements less reliable.

To check whether the ENZYMES local improvements were stable, we also ran a follow-up evaluation using the most promising settings suggested by the scan. The follow-up remained below the original full-suite ENZYMES cross results, so we do not replace the main table. Instead, the result supports a more careful interpretation: on the smallest dataset, validation noise can dominate local hyperparameter gains, and apparent improvements do not necessarily transfer to the 5-fold mean.

\subsection{Current Limitations}

The experiments are rigorous within the chosen benchmark scope, but they do not remove the core scope limitations of the current study.

The experiments remain structure-centric rather than fully omics-integrative. Accordingly, the strongest defensible claim of the paper is that CR-GNN is an effective biomolecular graph representation architecture with dataset-dependent cross-residual gains. The current evidence does not yet justify a broader claim about integrative omics prediction or direct discovery of novel genes, domains, or motifs, but the revised benchmark package still has a clear extension path toward plant-related graph inference settings.
