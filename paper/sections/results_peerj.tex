\section*{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section reports the final empirical evidence in three layers: the full benchmark, the targeted gate and residual-routing studies, and the exploratory sensitivity analyses. The main benchmark and the targeted ablations use the paper-facing \texttt{V3} five-fold results, whereas the older sensitivity scans are retained only as supporting trend analyses.

\subsection*{Overall benchmark performance}

The final benchmark evaluates six datasets, nine methods, four operators, and five outer folds under one unified protocol. For readability, the main text reports compressed summary tables over the active biological and supplementary datasets, while the complete matrix is reproduced in the appendix.

\subsection*{Results on the biological core datasets}

\textbf{Residual reuse remains the strongest default family in the reported suite.} Residual variants win PROTEINS, DD, ENZYMES, and AIDS. In particular, GraphResGNN is best on PROTEINS ($0.7223 \pm 0.0367$), ENZYMES ($0.3117 \pm 0.0746$), and AIDS ($0.9160 \pm 0.0121$), while NodeResGNN is best on DD ($0.7207 \pm 0.0259$).

\textbf{Cross-residual design is selectively strong, not universally dominant.} Cross variants still win completed datasets such as MUTAG and Mutagenicity. More importantly, the best cross variant beats PlainGNN on most completed datasets. Therefore, cross routing is clearly more than a design curiosity, but the evidence does not support the stronger claim that cross universally outperforms residual reuse.

\textbf{Node-level cross is more reliable than graph-level cross.} Across the completed datasets, NodeCrossGNN outperforms NodeResGNN more often than GraphCrossGNN outperforms GraphResGNN. This suggests that exchanging intermediate node-level states is the more robust cross-residual design in the present study, while graph-level cross routing is more dataset-sensitive.

\textbf{Plain stacking is no longer a credible final baseline.} PlainGNN remains competitive on PROTEINS and DD, but it never wins a dataset in the final suite and is usually surpassed by either a residual or a cross-residual variant.

\subsection*{Topic-focused results for PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES}

The topic-facing interpretation is anchored in the protein-oriented core datasets.

On \textbf{PROTEINS}, GraphResGNN is clearly strongest, indicating that repeated graph-level residual refinement is the most effective way to preserve useful global structure. The best cross model here is NodeCrossGNN at $0.6963 \pm 0.0433$, which improves slightly over NodeResGNN but remains below both PlainGNN and GraphResGNN.

On \textbf{DD}, NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN are nearly tied, with NodeResGNN retaining a narrow lead. This indicates that node-level hidden-state reuse is important on large sparse protein graphs, but cross exchange does not produce a decisive improvement over simpler residual reuse.

On \textbf{ENZYMES}, cross variants become more attractive. GraphCrossGNN reaches $0.3017 \pm 0.0750$ and NodeCrossGNN reaches $0.3000 \pm 0.0580$, both clearly above PlainGNN at $0.2450 \pm 0.0407$. Even so, GraphResGNN still remains the best model.

\subsection*{Results on the supplementary robustness datasets}

The supplementary package broadens the scope beyond the biological core without changing the main conclusion. NodeCrossGNN is strongest on MUTAG and Mutagenicity, whereas GraphResGNN remains strongest on AIDS. This pattern preserves the paper's main boundary claim: cross-residual interaction has selective practical value, but the residual family remains the more reliable default reference across the full suite.

\subsection*{Gate ablation results}

\input{../md/peerj_gate_tables.tex}

The gate ablations are designed to test whether adaptive control of residual injection is useful once the main benchmark has identified strong target configurations. On the strongest graph-level residual line, \texttt{AIDS + GraphResGNN + GINConv}, the comparison among learnable and fixed gates is the cleanest direct test of adaptive graph-level reuse. Table~\ref{tab:aids_gate_ablation} shows that the learnable gate is the strongest setting, although the fixed coefficient \texttt{0.5} remains very close. The cross-gate ablation extends the same comparison to six settings where a cross architecture is already locally favored.

The intended interpretation is constrained. The gate study does not need to show that learnable gating is best on every target. Instead, the relevant question is whether adaptive control is generally more robust than fixed injection strengths across the selected residual and cross-residual settings. Table~\ref{tab:cross_gate_ablation} shows a mixed result: learnable gating is best on three of the six cross-favored targets, while fixed gates remain competitive and can even be superior on the other three targets. This supports a robustness claim, not a universal-optimum claim.

\subsection*{Residual-routing results}

\input{../md/peerj_residual_tables.tex}

The residual-routing analysis is the main mechanism extension of the paper. Four representative targets compare learnable, top-k, and sparse routing modes under the same five-fold protocol. The clearest overall signal is that there is no universal winner. Table~\ref{tab:residual_routing_full} shows that \texttt{learnable} remains best on the strongest graph-level residual line (\texttt{AIDS + GraphResGNN + GINConv}), but sparse routing is strongest on three of the four representative targets, with \texttt{sparse\_0p05} emerging as the most consistent sparse setting.

This pattern supports a more precise conclusion than the architecture-only benchmark alone. The important question is not only whether historical information is reused, but also how aggressively that information is filtered before reuse. Table~\ref{tab:residual_routing_family} compresses the same result by routing family: the sparse family is strongest on three of the four representative targets, while the learnable family remains strongest on the best graph-level residual line.

\subsection*{Residual-parameter sweep results}

The additional routing settings \texttt{topk\_0p75} and \texttt{sparse\_0p1} refine the previous result rather than changing it. Table~\ref{tab:residual_routing_full} shows that the strongest top-k ratio varies by target, which indicates that top-k routing is not governed by one globally optimal retention level. The sparse sweep also shows a clear failure mode: aggressive sparsity can damage otherwise strong residual lines, especially on the strongest graph-level residual target, where \texttt{sparse\_0p1} drops to \texttt{0.8315 $\pm$ 0.0549}.

\subsection*{Exploratory sensitivity analysis}

Auxiliary sensitivity checks over depth, dropout, and learning rate on PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES do not materially change the main conclusion. On PROTEINS and DD, moderate tuning changes affect the gap between the two cross variants, but they do not overturn the broader conclusion that residual baselines remain the stronger default reference. On ENZYMES, the validation signal is visibly noisier, and promising local settings did not reproduce the original full-suite cross results under the final 5-fold average.
