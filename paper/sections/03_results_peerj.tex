\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section reports empirical evidence in four layers: the full benchmark, depth-scaling mechanism analyses, internal CR-GNN trade-off quantification, and targeted ablation studies. The main benchmark and ablations use stratified five-fold results reported as mean best test accuracy $\pm$ fold-level standard deviation; single-fold sensitivity analyses are reported as exploratory evidence only.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Overall benchmark performance}
% ============================================================

The final benchmark evaluates six datasets and nine methods (five CR-GNN variants plus four external baselines), with CR-GNN models using GCNConv and each baseline using its native operator, under five outer folds.

\input{../md/all_exp_tables.tex}

The three tables together reveal a nuanced landscape. Within the CR-GNN family, GraphResGNN is the strongest on PROTEINS, ENZYMES, and AIDS; NodeResGNN leads on PROTEINS. Among external baselines, GIN is particularly strong on MUTAG (0.8403) and AIDS (0.9145), while GraphSAGE leads on DD (0.7334) and Mutagenicity (0.8084). The presence of competitive external baselines reinforces the paper's central framing: the CR-GNN family is not claimed to be universally dominant, but rather provides a controlled design space for studying systematic trade-offs.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Results on the biological core datasets}
% ============================================================

The main biological benchmark results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:all_results_1}.

\textbf{PROTEINS.} Within the CR-GNN family, NodeResGNN leads numerically (0.7143 $\pm$ 0.0223), followed closely by GraphResGNN (0.7071 $\pm$ 0.0402). External baselines GraphSAGE (0.6954), GIN (0.6972), and JKNet (0.6954) cluster near the CR-GNN models. APPNP performs markedly worse (0.5966).

\textbf{DD.} GraphSAGE leads overall (0.7334 $\pm$ 0.0227), with GraphResGNN as the strongest CR-GNN variant (0.7275 $\pm$ 0.0144). Notably, the two cross-residual variants---GraphCrossGNN (0.7207) and NodeCrossGNN (0.7164)---both outperform NodeResGNN (0.7088), supporting the observation that cross-branch exchange is especially effective on large, sparse protein graphs.

\textbf{ENZYMES.} All models perform poorly in absolute terms, with GraphResGNN leading (0.3333 $\pm$ 0.1000). The CR-GNN family's relative ordering (GraphRes $>$ GraphCross $>$ NodeCross $\approx$ NodeRes $\approx$ Plain) is consistent with patterns on other datasets, though the 6-class, 600-graph setting creates a noisy regime that limits the strength of architecture-level conclusions.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Results on the supplementary robustness datasets}
% ============================================================

The supplementary robustness results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:all_results_2}.

\textbf{MUTAG.} GIN is the strongest model overall (0.8403 $\pm$ 0.0298), substantially ahead of all other methods. Within the CR-GNN family, NodeResGNN (0.7451) and NodeCrossGNN (0.7398) are competitive with GraphSAGE (0.7504). GraphResGNN performs worst (0.7238), consistent with the observation that aggressive representational reshaping is less suited to small molecular graphs.

\textbf{AIDS.} GIN (0.9145 $\pm$ 0.0415) and GraphResGNN (0.9090 $\pm$ 0.0194) are nearly tied, with GraphResGNN showing substantially lower variance. GraphSAGE (0.8800), NodeCrossGNN (0.8850), JKNet (0.8775), and GraphCrossGNN (0.8740) form a tight cluster.

\textbf{Mutagenicity.} GraphSAGE leads (0.8084 $\pm$ 0.0180), followed closely by GraphResGNN (0.8022 $\pm$ 0.0244) and GraphCrossGNN (0.7992 $\pm$ 0.0232). The top four models are separated by only 0.015, with overlapping standard deviations.

\subsection{Depth scaling and representation similarity}
% ============================================================

To understand the mechanism behind the benchmark patterns, we track hidden-state similarity (CKA and cosine) and per-layer gradient norms across $L=1$ through $L=8$ layers on PROTEINS under GCNConv.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_cka_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{CKA similarity between adjacent depth stages across layer counts $L=1$ through $L=8$. Each subplot shows one model; curves correspond to different layer counts. Higher CKA indicates greater representational continuity across depth.}
\label{fig:layer_cka_comparison}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_cosine_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{Cosine similarity between adjacent depth stages across layer counts $L=1$ through $L=8$. Negative values indicate anti-correlated representations between adjacent stages.}
\label{fig:layer_cosine_comparison}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:layer_cka_comparison} shows the CKA similarity between adjacent depth stages for each model, and Figure~\ref{fig:layer_cosine_comparison} shows the corresponding cosine similarity. Three patterns emerge. First, deeper stacking consistently reduces adjacent-stage similarity across all five architectures, confirming that depth-induced representation divergence is a general phenomenon. Second, at $L=2$--$3$, all architectures maintain relatively high CKA; at $L=8$, the ordering becomes NodeResGNN (0.968) $>$ GraphCrossGNN (0.952) $>$ PlainGNN (0.951) $>$ NodeCrossGNN (0.946) $>$ GraphResGNN (0.942). Third, and most importantly, the relationship between CKA and performance is the opposite of what a naive representational-stability hypothesis would predict: the model with the highest CKA (NodeResGNN) wins only 3 of 24 combinations, while the model with the lowest CKA (GraphResGNN) wins 12 of 24. This counter-intuitive pattern suggests that GraphResGNN's sequential block structure, by deliberately reshaping representations at each stage using reinjected graph-level context, produces more discriminative final representations precisely because of---rather than in spite of---the representational change between stages.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/exp/fig_supp_layer_gradient_comparison_PROTEINS_GCNConv.pdf}
\caption{Deepest-layer gradient L2 norm as a function of layer count ($L=1$ through $L=8$) for all five architectures on PROTEINS (GCNConv).}
\label{fig:layer_gradient_comparison}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:layer_gradient_comparison} shows that PlainGNN's deepest gradient norm degrades most rapidly with depth, while the residual family maintains more stable gradients. NodeResGNN and NodeCrossGNN maintain comparable gradient stability across all depths, confirming that cross-branch exchange does not introduce destructive gradient interference. GraphResGNN and GraphCrossGNN occupy intermediate positions.

\subsection{Performance trade-offs within the CR-GNN family}
% ============================================================

To isolate the systematic trade-offs among the five CR-GNN topologies, Table~\ref{tab:winner_summary} summarizes winner counts across all 24 dataset--operator combinations (GCNConv-only). GraphResGNN achieves the highest peak accuracy in 12 of 24 combinations (50\%). NodeCrossGNN is second with 6 wins (25\%), dominating DD across three of four operators (GATConv, SAGEConv, GINConv). NodeResGNN wins 3 combinations, PlainGNN wins 2 (both on MUTAG), and GraphCrossGNN wins 1.

Beyond win counts, the architectures differ systematically in cross-dataset consistency (Table~\ref{tab:consistency}). GraphCrossGNN, despite winning only one combination, is the most rank-stable design (rank std 0.75), never placing below fourth on any dataset. GraphResGNN, while the most frequent winner, drops to fifth on MUTAG (rank std 1.46). NodeCrossGNN occupies an intermediate consistency position (rank std 1.00) and is the closest competitor to the best model on average (mean gap 0.022).

The depth-scaling CKA results and the rank-consistency analysis thus reveal a three-way tension: GraphResGNN aggressively reshapes representations to achieve the highest peak accuracy but with inconsistent ranking; NodeResGNN preserves representations most faithfully (highest CKA) but with volatile performance; and GraphCrossGNN balances these forces to achieve the most consistent, though not peak, performance. The DD case further illustrates this trade-off: NodeCrossGNN wins under three of four operators, consistent with dual-branch node-level exchange being especially effective on large, sparse protein graphs where complementary parallel pathways preserve discriminative signals that a single branch would suppress. On smaller, denser graphs such as AIDS, GraphResGNN's sequential graph-conditioned block structure is more effective.

The gate ablations test whether adaptive control of residual injection is necessary for the observed architectural advantages. On the strongest graph-level residual target (\texttt{AIDS + GraphResGNN + GINConv}), the learnable gate is the strongest setting, though fixed coefficient 0.5 remains close. On the six cross-favored targets, learnable gating is best on three and fixed gates are best on the other three---indicating that cross-residual architectures are not overly sensitive to gate configuration, consistent with their greater rank stability.

Tables~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_main} and~\ref{tab:statistical_tests_supp} provide the complete paired-test matrix. Most architecture-level numerical gaps are modest relative to fold-level variation, confirming that differences between reuse topologies are real but conditional.

\subsubsection{Residual-routing results}

\input{../md/peerj_residual_tables.tex}

The residual-routing analysis adds a mechanism dimension to the trade-off framework. Four representative targets compare learnable, top-k, and sparse routing modes. Sparse routing with moderate threshold (0.05) is the most frequently optimal configuration, winning on three of four targets. Learnable dense routing remains best only on the strongest graph-level residual line. Two of the three sparse-favoring targets involve cross-residual architectures, suggesting that sparse filtering and cross-branch exchange form a particularly effective combination: sparsity suppresses weak residual channels, while the cross-branch pathway ensures that genuinely complementary information is preserved.

\subsubsection{Residual-parameter sweep results}

The additional routing settings refine rather than change the pattern. The strongest top-k ratio varies by target, confirming no globally optimal retention level. Aggressive sparsity (0.10) damages the strongest graph-level residual target, reinforcing that the benefit of sparsity is conditional on an appropriate threshold.

\subsection{Exploratory sensitivity analysis}

Auxiliary sensitivity checks over depth, dropout, and learning rate on PROTEINS, DD, and ENZYMES do not materially change the main conclusions. These checks are reported for transparency but should not be read as evidence at the same level as the repeated-split benchmark and targeted ablations.
