\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper compares five residual routing topologies under a controlled graph-classification protocol and finds that peak accuracy, cross-dataset consistency, and representational continuity are systematically traded off---no single architecture excels at all three. The main contribution is the controlled comparison framework that makes these trade-offs quantitatively visible.

Returning to the three research questions: (1) Cross-residual interaction helps most on large, sparse protein graphs (DD), where dual-branch exchange preserves discriminative signals that a single branch suppresses; graph-level residual propagation is stronger on smaller, denser graphs where global context dominates. (2) The ranking is mixed---GraphResGNN wins most frequently but fails badly on MUTAG; GraphCrossGNN never wins yet never disappoints---so the choice depends on whether peak accuracy or consistent reliability is the priority. (3) Routing strategy is a meaningful secondary design axis: sparse routing combined with cross-residual architectures is the most frequently optimal configuration.

The depth-scaling analysis on PROTEINS (GCNConv) reveals a ``CKA--performance paradox'' that warrants broader validation: the architecture with the highest representational continuity (NodeResGNN) achieves the fewest wins, while the architecture with the lowest continuity (GraphResGNN) achieves the most. This pattern suggests---but does not yet prove---that structured representational change between layers can be productive rather than destructive.

The strongest claims are supported by the stratified five-fold benchmark, the targeted five-fold ablations, and the $L=1$--$8$ depth-scaling analysis. The study addresses controlled graph-classification behavior on medium-scale biomolecular benchmarks; future work should test whether the CKA--performance paradox and the sparse-plus-cross-residual synergy generalize to larger graphs and richer biological inputs.
